# Product Manager Interview Playbook

> A reusable practice system for Product Manager interviews, with particular emphasis on product building, product design, growth, strategy, analytics, execution, estimation, and behavioural leadership.

**Research reviewed:** 18 July 2026  
**Audience:** Senior, Lead, Principal and experienced Product Manager candidates  
**Important:** each company's process, interview mix, and terminology can vary by role, level, product area, and location. Treat the recruiter’s briefing as the source of truth for a specific interview. This playbook describes recurring patterns, not a guaranteed question list.

## How to use this file

Use this as a practice operating system rather than a script library.

1. Select an archetype, not a memorised question.
2. Answer the prompt aloud in a timed session.
3. Let the interviewer interrupt and add constraints.
4. Score the reasoning, not just the final idea.
5. Record the follow-up questions that exposed a weakness.
6. Generate a new question with the same intent but a different surface context.

The goal is to become recognisably strong across unfamiliar problems. It is not to sound as though you have rehearsed a particular answer.

## 1. What the Product Manager interview is trying to assess

Product Manager interviews commonly assess the ability to bridge customer needs, technology, and business; guide products from conception to launch; work with engineering and other functions; use data to make decisions; and break complex problems into steps. Interview guidance commonly emphasises hypothetical and situational questions, structured thinking, ambiguity, user needs, metrics, market and competitor research, and cross-functional leadership.

This playbook groups the evaluation into six core components:

| Assessment area | What it means in practice | Typical prompt families |
|---|---|---|
| Product insights | Understand users, define a valuable problem, articulate a product vision and shape a coherent solution | Product design, product improvement, user problem, product vision |
| Strategic insights | Connect customer, market, competition, trends, business model and long-term choices | Growth, monetisation, pricing, market entry, competitive strategy |
| Analytical | Use evidence, metrics, estimation and causal reasoning to make decisions | Metric design, metric drop, experiment analysis, sizing, diagnosis |
| Cross-functional collaboration | Influence engineering, design, sales, marketing, legal and leadership through disagreement or pressure | Conflict, prioritisation, escalation, customer requests, incident response |
| Craft and execution | Turn direction into a shippable plan, manage trade-offs, launch, learn, iterate or sunset | Roadmaps, launch readiness, prioritisation, operating reviews, failure recovery |
| Leadership, responsibility and collaboration | Show curiosity, humility, integrity, resilience, initiative, low-ego collaboration and the ability to lead through ambiguity | Behavioural, leadership, influence, failure, ethics, learning |

These components overlap. A strong growth answer still needs product insight; a metric answer still needs strategy; a behavioural story still needs evidence of judgement and results.

### The deeper evaluation model

Across the categories, interviewers are usually asking four questions:

1. **Can this person find the right problem?**
2. **Can this person make a high-quality decision with incomplete information?**
3. **Can this person create alignment and get the work done?**
4. **Would this person raise the quality of the team and the product?**

For senior candidates, add a fifth:

5. **Can this person create leverage beyond one feature, squad or quarter?**

## 2. Interview process and what to verify with the recruiter

PM interview processes vary by company, role, level, product area, and location. Treat the target company's recruiter briefing and job description as the source of truth. This playbook provides recurring practice patterns, not a guaranteed process or question list.

### A practical process model

| Stage | What may happen | How to prepare |
|---|---|---|
| Application / recruiter screen | Background, motivation, role fit, level, location, and process briefing | Prepare a concise career narrative, motivation for the company and product area, and evidence of scope |
| Preliminary screen | One or more product cases, often product insight, strategy, analytics, or behavioural | Practise timed cases with live follow-ups |
| Interview loop | Several interviews covering a subset or all of the assessment areas; exact mix varies | Prepare across all question families even if a nominal focus is provided |
| Decision and follow-up | Structured feedback, debrief, references, team matching, or additional interviews may apply | Ask the recruiter what the current sequence and decision process are |

### Questions to ask the recruiter

Before the interview, clarify:

- Which level and product area is the role aligned to?
- How many interviews are in the loop and how long is each one?
- Which competency or assessment area is each interviewer expected to assess?
- Is there a technical, SQL, coding, prototyping, AI, or domain-specific component?
- Will the interview use a shared document, virtual whiteboard, or another tool?
- Are behavioural questions embedded in the cases or reserved for a separate conversation?
- What happens after the interview loop?

Do not assume that an online guide, candidate report, or old recruiter document reflects the exact loop you will receive.
## 3. How to classify a question before answering

Many structured PM prompts are deliberately ambiguous. The first skill is identifying the level of the question.

| If the prompt sounds like… | It is probably testing… | Start with… |
|---|---|---|
| “Design a product for…” | Product design / product insights | User, context, unmet need, success |
| “How would you improve…” | Product improvement / product sense | Product goal, user segment, pain point, opportunity |
| “How would you grow…” | Growth strategy | Baseline, growth objective, growth model, highest-leverage constraint |
| “Should the company enter…” | Business or product strategy | Market, company advantage, options, strategic fit, risks |
| “How would you monetise…” | Business model / pricing strategy | Value, payer, willingness to pay, adoption effects, trade-offs |
| “Usage dropped by…” | Analytics / root-cause diagnosis | Definition, segmentation, instrumentation, hypotheses, action |
| “How many…” | Estimation / analytical reasoning | Scope, equation, assumptions, arithmetic, sensitivity |
| “What would you do if…” | Execution or cross-functional leadership | Objective, stakeholders, options, decision rule, communication |
| “Tell me about a time…” | Behavioural / leadership | Specific situation, your judgement, actions, result, learning |

### A useful opening move

When the scope is unclear, say:

> “I’ll make a couple of assumptions so I can be concrete. I’ll first clarify the objective and user or business context, then identify the highest-leverage opportunity, compare a few options, and finish with an MVP and measures of success. If you intended a company-level strategy answer rather than a product-design answer, I’ll keep the solution at that level.”

This demonstrates structure without announcing a rigid framework.

## 4. Product sense and product design

### What the interviewer wants to see

- You begin with a real user and context rather than a feature.
- You can distinguish a symptom from an underlying problem.
- You choose a segment and explain why it matters.
- Your solution follows from the problem.
- You can generate more than one viable direction.
- You make a clear prioritisation decision.
- You can describe a usable first version, not only a vision.
- You connect user value to business or ecosystem value.
- You recognise trust, privacy, safety, accessibility and technical constraints.

### A reusable product-design answer spine

#### 1. Clarify the objective and constraints

Ask one or two high-value questions:

- Who is the target user or customer?
- Is the goal adoption, engagement, retention, revenue, task success or something else?
- Are we designing a new product, improving an existing one or entering a market?
- What time horizon or platform constraint should I assume?

Do not ask every possible question. Ask only what changes the decision.

#### 2. Establish the product or business context

State what you believe the product is for and what success would mean. For example:

> “I’ll optimise for helping occasional travellers plan with confidence, rather than maximising raw search volume.”

#### 3. Choose a user segment

Name a primary segment and explain the choice. A useful segment is behaviourally meaningful, not merely demographic.

Examples:

- first-time users rather than all users;
- time-poor commuters rather than all map users;
- small businesses with low technical capability rather than all advertisers;
- people with accessibility needs who are currently underserved.

#### 4. Identify jobs, pain points and moments of failure

Describe what the user is trying to accomplish, what makes it difficult, and how the current experience fails. Separate:

- functional need;
- emotional or confidence need;
- trust, safety or accessibility need;
- business or ecosystem constraint.

#### 5. Set a success metric before brainstorming

Choose one primary outcome and a small set of guardrails.

Example:

- Primary: percentage of target users completing the intended task successfully.
- Secondary: repeat use within 30 days, time to completion, quality rating.
- Guardrails: complaint rate, unsafe outcomes, latency, cost to serve, privacy incidents.

#### 6. Generate multiple solution directions

Produce three or four distinct approaches. Vary the mechanism, not just the colour of the interface.

Useful directions include:

- reduce effort;
- improve confidence or decision quality;
- personalise the experience;
- create a network or ecosystem effect;
- use automation or AI where it genuinely reduces work;
- create a new service layer or platform capability.

#### 7. Prioritise explicitly

Use a simple decision rule such as:

> “I’ll prioritise the option that solves the selected user’s most important pain, can reach enough users through the company’s existing distribution, and can be tested safely within one or two quarters.”

Then compare impact, reach, confidence, effort, strategic leverage and risk.

#### 8. Detail the MVP and learning plan

Explain:

- the user journey;
- what is included and excluded;
- how the experience works end to end;
- the first experiment or prototype;
- how you would learn before scaling.

#### 9. Close with trade-offs and next steps

Restate the user, problem, chosen solution, metric and main risk. Strong candidates also say what they would not build yet.

### Product design follow-up probes

Expect questions such as:

- Why this user rather than another?
- What evidence would change your mind?
- Why is this a problem worth solving for the company?
- What is the smallest version you could launch?
- How do you know the user would change behaviour?
- What would engineering say is difficult?
- What happens if adoption is high but quality is low?
- How would this work for a user with a disability or low connectivity?
- How could this be abused?
- What would you sunset to fund it?

## 5. 10× growth and product growth questions

### What “10×” is actually testing

“How would you grow X 10×?” is not primarily a creativity test. It tests whether you can:

- define the starting point and time horizon;
- understand the product’s growth model;
- distinguish incremental optimisation from a step-change bet;
- identify the binding constraint;
- choose a few high-leverage moves;
- sequence bets and experiments;
- preserve quality, trust and unit economics at scale.

The number is a forcing function. It asks you to escape the current mental model without abandoning commercial and user reality.

### 10× growth framework

#### 1. Define the objective

Clarify what is being grown:

- active users;
- successful tasks;
- revenue or gross profit;
- creator or developer supply;
- retention;
- customer value;
- geographic or segment penetration.

Also define the horizon and constraints.

#### 2. Write the growth equation

Choose a simple model:

```text
Outcome = Reach × Activation × Frequency × Retention × Value per user
```

For a marketplace:

```text
Transactions = Buyers × Purchase frequency × Conversion × Fulfilment success
```

For a subscription product:

```text
Revenue = Eligible market × Acquisition × Activation × Retention × Price
```

The exact equation is less important than making the levers visible.

#### 3. Diagnose the constraint

Ask where growth is currently blocked:

- insufficient awareness or distribution;
- weak activation;
- poor product value;
- low frequency;
- churn or trust failure;
- supply constraint;
- pricing or monetisation constraint;
- market size or regulatory constraint.

Do not propose ten initiatives before deciding which constraint matters most.

#### 4. Generate growth bets across horizons

Use three horizons:

| Horizon | Purpose | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Core optimisation | Improve the current funnel and experience | Onboarding, search quality, recommendations, retention, pricing tests |
| Adjacency | Extend the product into a nearby need or segment | New customer segment, partner channel, adjacent service, bundled offer |
| Step-change | Change the growth curve or business model | New platform, ecosystem, AI-native experience, new distribution or category |

#### 5. Prioritise the portfolio

Recommend a portfolio rather than a single magical bet:

- one or two near-term experiments that improve the core;
- one adjacency with a credible route to scale;
- one longer-term option that could create the step-change.

State which bet receives the most investment and why.

#### 6. Define leading indicators and kill criteria

For each bet, specify:

- leading indicator;
- expected mechanism;
- experiment or pilot;
- time to learn;
- scale condition;
- kill or pivot condition;
- guardrail for user trust, safety, quality and economics.

### Example answer structure

> “I’ll interpret 10× as increasing successful weekly use over three years, not just app installs. I’d first map the current growth equation and identify whether the constraint is reach, activation, retention, frequency or value. I’d then allocate the portfolio across core, adjacency and step-change bets. My initial thesis is that retention and frequency are more durable than buying reach, so I’d test a more valuable repeat-use workflow, expand into an underserved adjacent segment, and explore a platform or AI-enabled distribution bet. I’d scale only if the leading indicators improve without degrading quality, trust or unit economics.”

### 10× growth follow-up probes

- Why is your chosen lever the constraint?
- What is the size of the opportunity?
- Why would competitors not copy this?
- What would make users change behaviour?
- What is the business model?
- What would you stop doing?
- What could go wrong at 10× scale?
- How would you preserve product quality?
- Which leading metric would move first?
- What result after six months would cause you to abandon the idea?

## 6. Product and business strategy

### Strategy versus design

Design answers usually narrow from a user problem to a product experience. Strategy answers usually begin one level higher:

```text
Market change → company advantage → strategic options → choice → capabilities → roadmap
```

If asked whether the company should enter a market, do not start with a feature list. Explain why the market matters, what the company could uniquely do, which options exist and what the company should choose.

### Strategy framework

#### 1. Define the strategic objective

Examples:

- defend the core business;
- increase user value;
- expand revenue;
- reduce cost to serve;
- enter an adjacent market;
- create a new platform capability;
- improve ecosystem health.

#### 2. Understand the market

Cover the minimum relevant context:

- customer and user needs;
- market size and growth;
- competitive alternatives;
- technology or regulation shifts;
- value chain and economics;
- barriers to entry;
- likely future state.

#### 3. Identify durable company advantages

Consider:

- distribution;
- data and learning loops;
- brand and trust;
- technical capabilities;
- infrastructure;
- ecosystem relationships;
- installed base;
- regulatory or operational expertise;
- ability to bundle or cross-subsidise.

#### 4. Generate strategic options

Useful option types:

- deepen the core;
- improve monetisation;
- enter an adjacent segment;
- partner or acquire;
- build a platform or API;
- create a new product category;
- deliberately decline and focus resources elsewhere.

#### 5. Make the strategic choice

State a thesis and trade-offs:

> “I would pursue this option because it uses the company’s strongest advantage, solves a growing customer problem, and creates a reinforcing data or distribution loop. I would not pursue the other option now because it would require capabilities the company does not have or would dilute the core.”

#### 6. Translate strategy into a roadmap

Sequence:

1. validate the market and problem;
2. build the minimum capability or experience;
3. test adoption and economics;
4. establish defensibility or ecosystem leverage;
5. scale and integrate;
6. revisit the portfolio allocation.

#### 7. Define strategic metrics

Use a mix of:

- customer outcome;
- adoption and retention;
- revenue or cost impact;
- ecosystem health;
- strategic capability created;
- risk and trust guardrails.

### Strategy question variants

- Should the company enter a new category?
- How should the company respond to a powerful competitor?
- What is the next growth opportunity for YouTube?
- How would you monetise a free product?
- What should the company build for a new user segment?
- Would you buy, build or partner?
- Should the company launch a lower-priced product?
- What should the company stop doing?

## 7. Execution and craft

Execution questions test whether your strategy survives contact with reality.

### Execution answer spine

1. Re-state the objective and urgency.
2. Identify the decision owner and affected stakeholders.
3. Separate reversible from irreversible decisions.
4. Define the minimum information needed now.
5. Create options and explicit decision criteria.
6. Make or recommend the decision.
7. Align the team around scope, roles, milestones and risks.
8. Establish metrics, checkpoints and escalation paths.
9. Communicate what changed, why and what happens next.

### Common execution scenarios

| Scenario | Strong answer should cover |
|---|---|
| Engineering says a committed launch will slip | User/business impact, scope options, quality bar, decision rights, communication and re-plan |
| A senior stakeholder demands an unplanned feature | Understand the underlying need, quantify impact, compare against roadmap, protect team focus, offer a principled alternative |
| A launch is below target | Diagnose whether the issue is acquisition, activation, value, quality or measurement; choose a recovery loop |
| A critical bug affects customers | Containment, severity, customer communication, rollback or mitigation, root cause and prevention |
| Product needs to be sunset | Evidence, alternatives, customer migration, economics, stakeholder alignment and respectful closure |
| Teams disagree on requirements | Shared objective, decision criteria, trade-offs, owner, deadline and documented decision |

### Experimentation

A strong experiment answer covers:

- hypothesis and mechanism;
- eligible population and randomisation unit;
- treatment and control;
- primary metric;
- guardrails;
- sample size or power considerations at an appropriate level;
- experiment duration and novelty effects;
- segmentation and heterogeneous effects;
- decision rule;
- risks such as interference, bias, learning effects or metric gaming.

Do not treat statistical significance as product success. A statistically reliable change can still be commercially immaterial, harmful to a segment or damaging to trust.

### Estimation

Use this sequence:

1. Clarify the quantity and scope.
2. Define the population and time period.
3. Break the problem into a small equation.
4. State rounded assumptions.
5. Calculate aloud.
6. Sense-check the result.
7. Identify the most sensitive assumption.

Example:

```text
Annual bookings
= population × eligible users × intent rate × conversion × bookings per converter
```

The interviewer is evaluating structure, assumptions, arithmetic, checking and communication—not whether you know the hidden answer.

## 9. Behavioural, leadership and responsibility

### What makes a behavioural answer strong

Use a flexible **STAR-L** structure:

- **Situation:** only the context needed to understand the stakes.
- **Task:** your responsibility and the decision that mattered.
- **Action:** what you personally did, including judgement, influence and trade-offs.
- **Result:** measurable outcome and impact on users, business or team.
- **Learning:** what changed in your approach afterward.

For senior roles, make the leadership mechanism visible:

- How did you influence without authority?
- How did you create clarity?
- What disagreement did you handle?
- What did you decide with incomplete information?
- What did you sacrifice or deprioritise?
- How did you make the team or system better?

### Story bank themes

Prepare six to eight stories that can be adapted without changing the facts:

1. Ambiguous problem that became a clear product strategy.
2. Major customer or business outcome.
3. Cross-functional conflict resolved through influence.
4. Failure, missed target or wrong decision.
5. Executive disagreement or difficult trade-off.
6. Product launch or operational incident.
7. User insight that changed the roadmap.
8. Experiment or data analysis that changed your mind.
9. Ethical, trust, safety, privacy or accessibility decision.
10. Coaching, mentoring or leverage beyond your immediate team.

### Representative behavioural prompts

- Tell me about a time you influenced engineering to support a product decision.
- Tell me about a time you disagreed with a senior stakeholder.
- Describe a product decision you got wrong.
- Tell me about a time you had to deliver with incomplete information.
- Describe a time customer evidence conflicted with business pressure.
- Tell me about a time a launch or project went badly.
- Describe a time you had to say no.
- Tell me about a time you improved how a team worked.
- What is the most difficult feedback you have received?
- Tell me about a time you chose the ethical or user-protective path at a cost.

### Behavioural follow-up probes

- What was your specific contribution?
- What alternatives did you consider?
- What did the other person believe?
- What did you say or do, exactly?
- What would your engineering partner say about your approach?
- What metric changed?
- What did you learn that you still use?
- What would you do differently now?

## 11. Evaluation criteria and self-scorecard

Use a 1–4 scale after every practice session.

| Dimension | 1 — weak | 2 — developing | 3 — strong | 4 — exceptional |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Framing | Jumps to features or assumptions | Clarifies some context but misses the real objective | Defines scope, objective and relevant constraints | Reframes the problem insightfully and efficiently |
| User insight | Generic users and pain points | Plausible but shallow user understanding | Specific segment, job, pain and context | Finds a non-obvious insight with strong empathy |
| Strategic judgement | Feature list or vague ambition | Some trade-offs but no thesis | Clear options, rationale, priorities and risks | Connects market, company advantage, leverage and sequencing |
| Creativity | Obvious or copied ideas | A few differentiated ideas | Several credible, relevant options | Original idea that follows from the problem and scales |
| Analytical rigour | Unsupported claims | Some metrics or assumptions | Clear model, metrics, diagnosis or experiment | Prioritises evidence and makes uncertainty explicit |
| Execution | Generic “align stakeholders” language | Basic plan with missing decisions | Concrete ownership, scope, milestones and risks | Creates alignment and leverage under pressure |
| Communication | Rambling or opaque | Understandable but uneven | Structured, concise, adaptive | Makes complex reasoning feel simple and compelling |
| Seniority / leverage | Owns a feature or task | Owns a project | Owns an outcome across functions | Changes strategy, capability or portfolio-level direction |
| User trust and responsibility | Ignores risks | Mentions risks late | Includes meaningful guardrails | Makes trust, safety, privacy and inclusion part of the design |

### A simple readiness test

You are improving when you can answer a new prompt with:

- a clear thesis within the first few minutes;
- a specific user or business objective;
- two or three options rather than one idea dump;
- an explicit prioritisation decision;
- metrics or assumptions that connect to the decision;
- a credible MVP, experiment or next step;
- calm, useful responses to follow-ups;
- a concise close that restates the recommendation and trade-offs.

## 12. Follow-up probes: how to practise the real difficulty

The first prompt is only the opening move. Create difficulty by adding constraints in sequence.

### Probe ladder

1. **Clarify:** “Who is the user?” “What does success mean?”
2. **Challenge:** “Why this segment?” “Why not the obvious alternative?”
3. **Constraint:** “You have half the engineering capacity.”
4. **Trade-off:** “The best user outcome reduces revenue.”
5. **Evidence:** “Your primary metric improved, but a guardrail worsened.”
6. **Scale:** “Now assume 10× the traffic.”
7. **Stakeholder:** “Sales or leadership wants another direction.”
8. **Reflection:** “What would change your mind?”

### High-value interviewer moves

- Replace the target user.
- Change the time horizon.
- Remove a key company asset.
- Introduce a competitor.
- Add a privacy, safety or regulatory concern.
- Give a metric result that contradicts the initial hypothesis.
- Ask what to stop doing.
- Ask for the first experiment and the kill criterion.

## 13. Anti-patterns and how to correct them

| Anti-pattern | What it signals | Correction |
|---|---|---|
| Framework recital | Memorised process without judgement | Use a light structure and make decisions in your own words |
| Feature dumping | No problem definition or prioritisation | Name the user, problem and objective before ideas |
| Solving the wrong level | Product answer to a company-strategy prompt | Identify whether the question is user, product, portfolio or market level |
| One perfect idea | Low exploration and weak trade-off thinking | Offer a small set of distinct options, then choose |
| “AI” as the strategy | Technology-led thinking | Explain the user problem, mechanism, feasibility and guardrails |
| Vanity metrics | Activity mistaken for value | Use an outcome metric plus diagnostics and guardrails |
| False precision | Assumptions hidden behind exact numbers | Round assumptions and identify sensitivity |
| Generic collaboration | Seniority not demonstrated | Explain the conflict, decision rule, influence mechanism and result |
| Refusing to make a call | Analysis without leadership | State the recommendation and what evidence would change it |
| Over-indexing on domain expertise | Failure to reason from first principles | State assumptions, use transferable business logic and ask focused questions |
| Ignoring the downside | Product optimism without responsibility | Include trust, safety, privacy, accessibility, quality and economics |
| Speaking only about the team | Unclear personal ownership | Use “we” for the team and “I” for your decisions and actions |
| Overlong answers | Poor prioritisation and weak executive communication | Signpost, answer the question asked, and close decisively |

## 14. Reusable question archetypes

The archetype is the stable practice unit. The surface context can change without changing the underlying intent or difficulty.

| Archetype | Core intent | Difficulty levers |
|---|---|---|
| Design for a user | User empathy, problem framing, creativity, prioritisation | Familiar vs unfamiliar user; clear vs ambiguous constraints |
| Improve an existing product | Product judgement and diagnosis | Mature product, competing segments, trust or technical limits |
| Find the next growth curve | Growth model, strategic insight, leverage | Saturated market, low data, constrained budget, 10× target |
| Enter or avoid a market | Strategy, company advantage, trade-offs | Competitive threat, weak capability, adjacent vs unrelated market |
| Monetise responsibly | Business model and user value | Free product, pricing sensitivity, ad load, trust constraint |
| Diagnose a metric change | Analytical reasoning and action | Unknown instrumentation, multiple segments, conflicting metrics |
| Estimate a market or usage level | Decomposition, assumptions, sensitivity | Sparse data, unusual unit, two-sided market |
| Design an experiment | Causal thinking and decision quality | Interference, low traffic, multiple outcomes, novelty effects |
| Launch under pressure | Execution and judgement | Slipping engineering, poor dogfood, executive visibility |
| Resolve stakeholder conflict | Influence and prioritisation | Sales pressure, senior disagreement, strategic customer |
| Respond to a failure | Ownership, learning and resilience | Public incident, missed target, ethical dimension |
| Decide whether to sunset | Portfolio judgement and communication | Loyal users, sunk cost, migration burden, strategic dependency |

### Archetype invariants

When generating a new question, preserve:

- the same primary competency;
- the same ambiguity level;
- the same number of meaningful trade-offs;
- the same expected answer length;
- the same need for a recommendation or decision;
- the same follow-up pressure.

Change the surface context, users, product, industry or constraint so the candidate must reason rather than recall.

### Generator prompt for a full practice session

```text
Run a realistic senior Product Manager interview.

Rules:
- Give me one prompt from the selected archetype only.
- Do not reveal the competency, framework or ideal direction.
- Wait for my answer before giving follow-ups.
- Ask one follow-up at a time.
- Use at least four follow-ups, including one constraint, one trade-off and one metric challenge.
- Interrupt only when a real interviewer would.
- Do not rescue me unless I am genuinely stuck.
- At the end, score framing, user insight, strategy, analytics, execution,
  communication, seniority and responsibility from 1–4.
- Give evidence for every score.
- Identify the single highest-value improvement.
- Generate a different question of equivalent intent for the next session.
```

## 16. Practice plan

### Four-week baseline

| Week | Focus | Output |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Product insights and design | Four timed cases, two product teardowns, one accessibility-focused case |
| 2 | Growth and strategy | Four growth/strategy cases, one market map, one monetisation case |
| 3 | Analytics, estimation and execution | Six mixed cases, including metric drops, experiments and launch scenarios |
| 4 | Behavioural and full-loop simulation | Story bank, two full mocks, targeted re-practice of weak archetypes |

### Practice balance

For a senior PM candidate, a useful starting mix is:

- 30% product insights / design;
- 20% growth and strategy;
- 20% analytics and estimation;
- 15% execution and collaboration;
- 15% behavioural and leadership.

Adjust the mix once the recruiter confirms the role-specific loop.

### Session review questions

After every case, write down:

1. What was the real question beneath the wording?
2. What assumption most affected my answer?
3. Did I make a clear choice?
4. Did the solution follow from the user or business problem?
5. Which metric would move first?
6. What did the follow-up expose?
7. What would a more senior answer have added?
8. What single behaviour will I change next time?

## 17. Sources and research notes

Use authoritative, role-specific sources when available:

- the target company's job description and recruiter briefing;
- the target company's published interview guidance, if available;
- product documentation, public metrics definitions, and reputable market research;
- established Product Management interview resources for additional prompt variety.

Treat online question lists and candidate reports as practice inputs, not predictions. Exact interview loops, competencies, terminology, and decision processes vary by company, role, level, product area, and location. Do not claim that this playbook represents any particular company's official process or hiring bar.

The assessment rubric is intentionally cross-company: product insight, strategic insight, analytical rigour, cross-functional collaboration, craft and execution, leadership and responsibility, and seniority/leverage where relevant.
## One-page answer checklist

Before finishing any case, check:

- Did I answer the question at the right level?
- Did I clarify the objective and constraints?
- Did I identify a meaningful user, customer or business problem?
- Did I make assumptions visible?
- Did I generate more than one credible option?
- Did I prioritise and explain why?
- Did I connect the decision to metrics, economics and risks?
- Did I include trust, safety, privacy, accessibility or quality where relevant?
- Did I describe the first test, launch step or learning loop?
- Did I make a clear recommendation?

The answer should leave the interviewer with a clear impression: **this person can find the important problem, make a thoughtful decision, create alignment and turn strategy into measurable user value at scale.**
